1. Overview

This page explores the relationship between PV loop mechanics and the risk of MCS escalation (ECMO, LVAD, transplant, or post-implant arrest). The key metric is Ees/Ea (ventriculo-arterial coupling ratio), which measures how well the left ventricle's contractility matches the arterial afterload.

Interpretable Model A focused logistic regression was trained using only PV loop variables (Ees, Ea, Ees/Ea, ESP, EDP, Pmax). Logistic regression is the correct choice here because MCS Escalation is a binary outcome (yes/no), not a continuous number. Linear regression would predict meaningless values outside the 0–1 probability range.

2. Ees/Ea Threshold Badge

For bedside triage, we define three zones based on pre-implant Ees/Ea:

ZoneEes/Ea RangeMeaningAction
Poor Coupling < 0.40 Contractility is low relative to afterload. The LV is struggling to overcome vascular resistance. High suspicion for escalation. Pre-position ECMO cart. Alert cardiac surgery early.
Borderline 0.40 – 0.60 Coupling is marginal. Some patients tolerate Impella alone; others need escalation. Increase monitoring frequency. Have RV inotropes and vasodilators ready.
Adequate > 0.60 Contractility and afterload are reasonably matched. Standard Impella support and routine surveillance.

These thresholds are clinical rules, not predictions. They summarize observed patterns in the cohort and should be used alongside the full predictive model and clinical judgment.

3. Scatter Plot: Ees/Ea vs Escalation

How to Read This Plot

X-axis: Ees/Ea (ventriculo-arterial coupling ratio). Lower values mean the LV is weak relative to the arterial resistance it faces.

Y-axis: Probability of MCS Escalation, ranging from 0 (definitely will not escalate) to 1 (definitely will escalate).

  • Green circles — Patients who did not need escalation (ECMO, LVAD, transplant, or arrest). They cluster mostly on the right side (higher Ees/Ea).
  • Red X's — Patients who did escalate. They appear more on the left side (lower Ees/Ea), though there is overlap.
  • Blue curve — Logistic regression fit. It is S-shaped, not straight. At very low Ees/Ea the probability flattens near 1; at very high Ees/Ea it flattens near 0.
  • Yellow dashed line (0.40) — The "Poor Coupling" threshold. To the left of this line, most patients have elevated escalation probability.
  • Green dashed line (0.60) — The "Adequate Coupling" threshold. To the right of this line, escalation probability is generally low.

Clinical takeaway: Lower Ees/Ea is directionally associated with higher escalation risk, but the relationship is moderate (AUC 0.59 for Ees/Ea alone). Many patients with low coupling never escalate, and some with high coupling do. This is why the PV-loop-only model is a screening tool, not a definitive predictor.

4. PV-Loop-Only Logistic Regression

Model trained on only PV loop variables to predict MCS Escalation. This is intentionally narrow — it tells us how much escalation risk is explainable from ventricular mechanics alone, before adding echo, labs, or inotropes.

Coefficients (Standardized)

Red bars push the prediction toward escalation. Green bars push it away. All features were z-score standardized, so coefficients are directly comparable.

How to Read the Coefficients

What is a coefficient? In logistic regression, a coefficient tells you how much the log-odds of escalation change when the feature increases by one standard deviation.

  • Positive coefficient (red bar): Higher values of this feature push the prediction toward escalation.
  • Negative coefficient (green bar): Higher values push the prediction away from escalation.
  • Odds Ratio (OR): For every 1-SD increase in the feature, the odds of escalation multiply by the OR. OR > 1 means risk increases; OR < 1 means risk decreases.

Example interpretation: Ees/Ea has a coefficient of −0.65 and OR of 0.52. This means: for every 1-standard-deviation decrease in Ees/Ea, the odds of escalation roughly double (because 1/0.52 ≈ 1.92). In plain language: worse ventriculo-arterial coupling → higher escalation risk.

Important caveat: Because all features were standardized, the coefficient magnitude reflects relative importance within this 6-feature model. Ees/Ea has the largest magnitude here, but when placed alongside 173 other clinical variables in the full model, its rank drops to #31.

Why Logistic Regression — Not Linear Regression

MCS Escalation is a binary outcome (yes/no). Linear regression is the wrong tool for three reasons:

  1. Predictions can be nonsensical. A linear model might output −0.3 or 1.4 for a probability. Probabilities must stay between 0 and 1. Logistic regression enforces this naturally via the S-shaped sigmoid function.
  2. It assumes a straight-line relationship. Biology is not linear. When Ees/Ea is very low, escalation probability plateaus near 1. When it is very high, it plateaus near 0. In the middle, the relationship curves. A straight line cannot capture this.
  3. Statistical assumptions are violated. Linear regression assumes residuals are normally distributed with constant variance. With a binary outcome, residuals are either 0 or 1 minus the prediction — they are not normal, and their variance changes with the predicted value. This makes p-values and confidence intervals unreliable.

5. SHAP Explainability

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) shows how much each feature contributed to the full escalation model (RandomForest, AUC 0.95). The plots below reveal where Ees/Ea ranks among all features.

How to Read the SHAP Summary Plot

This is a beeswarm plot. Each dot is one patient. The plot is ordered by feature importance (top = most important).

  • X-axis (SHAP value): How much this feature pushed the prediction. Positive = pushed toward escalation. Negative = pushed away from escalation.
  • Dot color: The actual value of the feature for that patient. Red = high value. Blue = low value.
  • Clustering: If red dots cluster on the right side of a feature, that means higher values of that feature are associated with higher escalation risk.

Example: If "pre_lvedd" is near the top with red dots on the right, it means larger pre-implant LVEDd is associated with higher predicted escalation probability. If "ees_ea" has blue dots on the right, it means lower Ees/Ea (blue = low) pushes the prediction toward escalation (right = positive SHAP).

Key takeaway: Ees/Ea ranks #31 out of ~179 features. It contributes, but it is not among the top drivers. The strongest predictors in the full model are inotrope count, pre-implant LVEDd, delta hemoglobin, and post-implant furosemide dose.

How to Read the SHAP Dependence Plot

This plot isolates only Ees/Ea and shows how its SHAP contribution changes as the raw value changes.

  • X-axis: The actual Ees/Ea value for each patient (standardized).
  • Y-axis: The SHAP value for Ees/Ea for that patient. Positive = pushed toward escalation. Negative = pushed away.
  • Color: A second feature (often the one most correlated with Ees/Ea). It reveals interaction effects.

What to look for: A downward trend from left to right means lower Ees/Ea → higher SHAP → higher escalation risk. If the color gradient shows a pattern (e.g., all red dots are on the top right), that means Ees/Ea interacts with that second feature.

Clinical takeaway: The dependence plot tells you whether the Ees/Ea effect is consistent across patients or whether it only matters in combination with something else (e.g., high afterload, low contractility).

6. Per-Patient SHAP Breakdown

For each patient with PV loop data, the table shows the raw Ees/Ea value, the SHAP contribution of Ees/Ea to the escalation prediction, and the final predicted probability.

How to Read Each Column

  • Patient: Name or MRN of the patient.
  • Ees/Ea: The raw pre-implant ventriculo-arterial coupling ratio. Lower = worse coupling.
  • Threshold Zone: A bedside color code based on the raw Ees/Ea value.
    Poor = < 0.40 (high suspicion)
    Borderline = 0.40–0.60 (watch closely)
    Adequate = > 0.60 (standard care)
  • Ees/Ea SHAP: How much this specific patient's Ees/Ea value contributed to the full escalation model prediction.
    + → escalation means this patient's Ees/Ea pushed the model toward predicting escalation.
    − → no escalation means it pushed the model away.
    The magnitude tells you how strongly it pushed. A SHAP of +0.15 is a larger push than +0.02.
  • Prediction: The full model's predicted probability of escalation for this patient, expressed as a percentage. This is not based on Ees/Ea alone — it incorporates all 179 features.
  • Actual: What really happened. Escalated = needed ECMO, LVAD, transplant, or had arrest. Not = none of those.

How to use this table clinically:

  1. Sort by Ees/Ea to find patients with the worst coupling. Check if their SHAP values are positive (the model noticed).
  2. Look for patients with high Prediction % but negative Ees/Ea SHAP. This means the model predicted escalation, but Ees/Ea was not the reason — something else (e.g., inotrope count, LVEDd) drove the prediction.
  3. Compare Prediction vs Actual to find false positives (model worried, but patient was fine) and false negatives (model missed an escalation). These cases teach you the model's blind spots.
